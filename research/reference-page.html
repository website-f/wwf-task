      We talk a lot about tiger prey, but what animals do tigers eat?

      The most important tiger prey are often ungulates, which are mammals with hooves, such as large deer, wild cattle, and wild pigs. Let’s take a look at some of the places where tiger prey are found.

      Sambar deer

      Sambar deer are one of the most important tiger prey species. They are often abundant in tiger reserves in India and Nepal Indian. They can also be found across Southeast Asia but are much rarer in the region due to hunting. The IUCN lists sambar deer as Vulnerable.

      Wild Pig

      Wild pigs are the most widely distributed tiger prey species and can be commonly found across Asia and occur in every landscape in which tigers are found. On the island of Sumatra (Indonesia) they are one of the most important tiger prey species. Female wild pig can have up to two litters of piglets per year, with 4-8 piglets per litter.

      Banteng

      One of the larger tiger prey species is the banteng, these are large wild cattle and are classified as Endangered by the IUCN. Historic hunting and a current snaring crisis in Southeast Asia have caused a decline in their populations. In Thailand, there are signs of  hope with populations increasing.

      A recently published paper showed that banteng populations in Huai Kha Khaeng, in the core of Thailand’s Western Forest Complex and the most important site for tigers in Southeast Asia, have doubled in the past 15 years. This is a result of strong law enforcement and a long-term investment into protection of a source tiger site.

      Bukhara deer

      In Central Asia a different set of species are important for tiger prey including bukhara deer. This is the Central Asian subspecies of the widespread red deer and populations are increasing thanks to conservation efforts. Restoring healthy bukhara deer numbers will be key to the success of a landmark tiger reintroduction project in the country. Tigers became extinct in Kazakhstan over 70 years ago and are planned to be reintroduced in the next few years.

      Nilgai

      Commonly found in India, Nilgai are the largest antelope found in Asia. Their thin legs, large torso, wide neck, and small head make them unique. Mostly found in India and some parts of Nepal, this tiger prey weighs in at roughly 100-288 kg depending whether it’s male or female.

      CHITAL

      Also known as spotted deer, chital are an important and abundant prey species for tigers in India and Nepal. They can also be found in Bangladesh and Bhutan and are listed as Least Concern by the IUCN.

      These are just a few examples of the many tiger prey species, other important ungulates for tigers in parts of their range include gaur, roe deer, sika deer, hog deer, muntjac, barasingha, and eld’s deer.

      Thanks to research we know one tiger needs to eat about 50 ungulates a year.

      But, in order for there to be 50 prey per year for one tiger, there needs to be roughly 500 prey and this 10% off take ensures populations of prey are not depleted over time.

      And for there to be 500 prey in a tiger’s territory, a healthy and connected habitat is essential.

      Ungulates are not just important for the survival of tigers, they’re also a key part of the wider ecosystem. They help to keep vegetation under control and play an important role in dispersing seeds across landscapes. Deer and wild cattle churn up water holes and these larger ungulates could also contribute to carbon cycling.

      Ungulates are also prey for other carnivores such as leopards, dhole, and clouded leopards. Having sufficient numbers doesn’t just help these cats, but it can help to reduce human-wildlife conflict. With enough prey to eat carnivores are less likely to stray from their habitat in search of alternative sources of food.

          In reality, tiger prey densities are generally low across Asia. 

          Historic poaching has led to a decline in ungulate populations across Asia. In Southeast Asia the decline is particularly severe as a snaring crisis has striped many of the region’s forests of their wildlife. Consumption of ungulates in the region continues to drive an unsustainable demand for wild meat.

          Diseases, such as African Swine Fever, are also having detrimental impacts on wild pig populations in Asia. In the Belum-Temengor Forest Complex in Malaysia, the disease caused high levels of pig mortality in one of the last tiger strongholds in the country.

              Securing a future for ungulates in tiger landscapes

              WWF is working with governments, local partners and communities across tiger landscapes to support the conservation of ungulates.

              WWF-Thailand is supporting the government of Thailand with releasing captive bred sambar deer into the country’s tiger landscapes. Between 2021-2024, 114 sambar deer were released and more releases are planned over the next few years. WWF is also working to restore grassland habitat in tiger landscapes which is key to the survival of sambar deer in the country. Sambar deer have also been released in neighbouring Malaysia to boost prey densities there too.

              WWF-Bhutan is supporting the Royal Government of Bhutan with SMART, a monitoring tool for people (such as rangers and community anti-poaching patrol teams) working to keep wildlife safe from poachers. This enables anti-poaching patrol teams to log the coordinates of illegal activity, upload photos, and build a picture of the threats in the landscape, helping to keep tigers and their prey safe.

              Ungulates and communities can come into conflict if animals feed on crops. Across Asia wild pigs are one of the most pervasive causes of human wildlife conflict. In countries such as India and Nepal interventions to reduce conflict between communities and ungulates are underway. Approaches to increase tolerance and encourage coexistence between communities and ungulates are long-term solutions that WWF is working on implementing across the tiger’s range. Interventions such as fences can provide short-term relief by preventing ungulates accessing crops, however a holistic approach to human-wildlife conflict is needed to ensure coexistence between people and ungulates.

              Another aspect to WWF’s ungulate conservation is reducing the demand for tiger prey. Ungulates are directly hunted for their meat, medicinal purposes, and for trophies. This is driving a reduction in their populations, mainly across Southeast Asia. WWF’s Zero Wild Meat Campaign targeted Cambodia, Laos, and Viet Nam where consumption of wild meat heavily threatens the survival of wildlife populations, fuels transboundary and domestic wildlife crime, and significantly increases the risk of zoonotic spillover from animals to people. Behaviour change campaigns take longer to see success than some landscape conservation interventions, but the campaign saw promising results in its effort to shift consumer behaviour. As part of the new WWF Tiger Strategy we will be implementing targeted evidence based behavioural change to reduce consumption of ungulate tiger prey in at least five important tiger landscapes.

              Protecting and restoring tiger prey is critical for ecosystem health and for the future of wild tigers across Asia. Governments, especially in Southeast Asia, need to prioritise ungulate conservation and ensure sufficient funding is available to achieve impact at scale. Because a future without these hoofed mammals is a future without tigers.
